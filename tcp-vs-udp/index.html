TCP vs UDP

“Reliable” has a price. Sometimes it's a frozen video call.

EncoreEncore sends ticket PDFs that must arrive perfectly, and now streams live concerts where a frame that arrives late is useless. On a bad mobile connection some packets get lost. TCP and UDP handle that loss in opposite ways.

    TCP

    A connection with a handshake, acknowledgements, retransmission and ordering. The app gets a perfect stream of bytes. When a packet is lost, everything after it waits (head-of-line blocking).

    UDP

    Fire-and-forget packets: no handshake, no retries, no ordering. Lost packets stay lost, and late ones aren't waited for. The app decides what to do, which is great when old data is worthless.

    The one difference

    TCP waits until it has every byte in order; UDP hands over what arrived, right now. For files, web pages and payments you want TCP. For live video, voice and games, a late packet is as bad as a lost one, so UDP (with the app filling the gaps) wins.

    Complete + ordered
    TCP
    Handshake, acknowledgements, retransmission, ordering, congestion control. HTTP/1 and HTTP/2, databases, SSH and email all use it.
    
    
    Fast + lossy
    UDP
    Just packets. Used by DNS, video calls, games and live streaming, and by QUIC, which builds its own reliability on top (page 23).
    
    
    The cost
    Head-of-line blocking
    One lost TCP packet delays everything behind it, even data that already arrived.
    
  
    
  
    The tradeoff
    TCP gives you correctness for free and charges you in latency when the network is bad. UDP gives you low latency and makes you handle loss, ordering and congestion yourself. That's why most apps use TCP, and why the ones that need real-time behavior use UDP with their own protocol on top.
