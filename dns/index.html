    The one difference

    DNS changes don't “propagate”: every cache just keeps the old answer until its TTL expires. A long TTL means fewer lookups and faster pages, and slow changes. Lower the TTL a day before a planned move (so old long-TTL copies expire first), then switch, then raise it again.

    DNS in one line

    DNS turns a name into an address by asking a chain of servers (root → top-level domain → the domain's own server), and every answer is cached along the way for as long as its TTL says.

    The whole page on one screen
    
  
    
    
    Chain
    Resolution
    Browser → OS → recursive resolver → root → TLD → authoritative server. Only a cold lookup walks the whole chain.
    
    
    Cache
    TTL
    Every answer carries a TTL. Every layer caches it that long, which is why most lookups take 0 ms, and why changes are slow.
    
    
    Records
    A, CNAME, MX…
    A: name → IPv4 address. AAAA: IPv6. CNAME: an alias for another name. MX: where email goes. TXT: verification and policies.
    
  
    
  
    The tradeoff
    Long TTLs cut lookup traffic and latency but make changes and failovers slow; short TTLs make changes fast but put more load on your DNS servers. Use hours for records that rarely change, and minutes for records you may need to move in an emergency.
